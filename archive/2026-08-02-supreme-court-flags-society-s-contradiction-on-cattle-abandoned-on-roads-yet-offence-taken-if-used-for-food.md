In a pointed observation that has sparked widespread discussion, the Supreme Court of India on Friday highlighted what it called a deep contradiction in society’s treatment of cattle. The Court noted that people routinely abandon animals once their economic usefulness ends and allow them to wander freely on roads with little concern for their safety or the safety of the public, while the same society takes strong offence if those animals are used for food.

The remarks came in a judgment delivered by a Bench of Justice Sanjay Karol and Justice N. Kotiswar Singh in Nisha v. Municipal Council, Sangrur. The case arose from the death of a man attacked by a stray bull in Sangrur, Punjab, in 2007. While awarding the widow ₹15 lakh in compensation, the Court used the opportunity to examine the larger and growing problem of stray cattle on Indian roads and highways.

The Bench observed that ideally, those who choose to rear animals should care for them throughout their lives. Reality, however, is different. Many cattle found roaming public spaces are animals originally kept for milk, labour or other economic purposes. Once productivity declines, they are often left to fend for themselves.

#### The Court put the societal contradiction in clear terms:

<p>“We have already touched upon the issue of animals being let go as their utility dwindled. In a perfect world, this would not be the case. All those who have chosen to bring an animal home shall see to it that they are looked after for their whole life. But the reality is that we live in a deeply flawed, human world.</p>
<p>Perhaps it is a feature of this flawed world that, on the one end, we let them roam about without caring for their safety, and, on the other, find it deeply offensive if an individual uses them to secure a full stomach for himself or his family.”</p>

The judges distinguished between pets and livestock, noting that most cattle on roads are not companion animals but animals reared for a specific economic purpose and then abandoned. They acknowledged the financial difficulties faced by many farmers and dairy owners who may lack the means to maintain unproductive cattle. Yet the Court was firm: economic hardship cannot justify turning public roads into dumping grounds for animals.

“Cattle are not meant to be natural speed breakers, placed willy-nilly on the National Highways, roads and streets,” the Bench remarked. Official data placed before the Court showed that more than 1,300 people lose their lives every year in animal-related incidents, underscoring that the problem is neither rare nor minor.

The Court also pointed out that while 24 states have laws regulating or prohibiting cattle slaughter, the corresponding provisions meant to protect cattle and prevent abandonment are poorly implemented. Protection on paper, it suggested, means little without effective enforcement and infrastructure.

To address the crisis, the Supreme Court recommended a series of practical measures for the Centre and state governments. These include:

▪️Effective implementation of existing cattle protection laws

▪️Creation of a mechanism to compensate victims of stray cattle accidents

▪️Mandatory tagging and digital identification of cattle

▪️Holding owners responsible for the safe transfer of animals they no longer wish to keep to authorised shelters

▪️Appointment of nodal officers to oversee tagging, digital records and the functioning of animal shelters

The Court directed that a copy of the judgment be sent to the Chief Secretaries of all States and Union Territories for consideration of these recommendations.

The observations strike at a sensitive cultural and economic intersection. On one side lies the reality of farmers who cannot afford to feed animals that no longer produce milk or work. On the other lies a societal and legal framework that treats the same animals as sacred once they become unproductive, while simultaneously failing to provide adequate shelters or systems to care for them. The result is visible every day: cattle on highways, accidents, injuries, deaths, and a continuing cycle of abandonment followed by outrage.

By calling out the contradiction so directly, the Supreme Court has shifted the conversation from abstract sentiment to concrete responsibility of owners, of governments, and of Particular society. Whether the recommendations lead to meaningful change will depend on how seriously states treat the problem of stray cattle not merely as an animal welfare issue, but as a question of public safety and honest policy.
